Do brain games make you smarter? What the science says

By Noam · 26 November 2026 · 7 min read

Lots of apps promise that a few minutes of puzzles a day will make you smarter, sharper or better at school. Puzzle games are great fun, and this site has plenty of them. But it's worth being honest about what they can and can't do. Here's what scientists have actually found.

You get better at what you practise

This part is clear: if you play a memory game every day, you'll get much better at that memory game. Practise mental maths and your mental maths improves. Practise typing and you type faster. Skills improve with practice, and games are a fun way to practise.

The big question: does it spread?

The harder question is called transfer: does getting better at one game make you better at other things, like reading, schoolwork or remembering where you left your keys? Here the research is much less exciting. Large reviews of brain-training studies have found that improvements usually stay close to the task that was practised. Getting better at a memory-grid game doesn't, on its own, seem to make people much better at unrelated tasks.

In 2016, the company behind one popular brain-training app agreed to pay a settlement to the US Federal Trade Commission, which said its adverts had claimed more than the science could support. Since then, most researchers have been careful to say that brain games are fun and can build specific skills, but are not a magic boost for intelligence.

So are brain games pointless?

Not at all. They're useful when the skill you practise is the skill you want:

  • Maths facts: games like Math Blitz are a fun way to make times tables automatic, which really does help in school maths.
  • Typing: Typing Rain practises the exact skill of typing.
  • Spelling and words: word games help with spelling patterns and vocabulary.
  • Logic: puzzles like Sudoku teach you to reason step by step and check your work, which is a useful habit.

And of course, enjoying a challenge is a perfectly good reason to play.

What does help your brain?

The things with the strongest evidence aren't apps at all:

  • Sleep: your brain stores new memories while you sleep.
  • Exercise: physical activity is good for attention and mood.
  • Learning real skills: a new language, a musical instrument, coding or a sport stretch your brain in many directions at once.
  • Reading: builds vocabulary and knowledge that helps with almost everything.

The bottom line

Play puzzle games because they're fun and because they help with the specific skills they practise. Just don't expect a game to replace sleep, exercise or learning things for real. A good balance is a few games, plus plenty of everything else.
