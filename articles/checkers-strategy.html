Checkers strategy: 7 tips to beat the computer

By Noam · 25 November 2026 · 6 min read

Checkers (called draughts in Britain) is played on the dark squares of an 8 by 8 board with twelve pieces each. Pieces move diagonally forward, jump to capture, and become kings when they reach the far side. It's simple enough to learn in minutes but deep enough that it took computer scientists decades to solve: in 2007 a team led by Jonathan Schaeffer showed that with perfect play from both sides, the game is a draw. Here's how to play a lot better than "perfect play is a draw" suggests.

1. Remember: jumps are compulsory

In standard checkers, if you can capture, you must. This is the most important rule for strategy, because it means you can force your opponent to move a piece where you want it. Offer one piece so that their forced jump lands them where you can jump two back.

2. Keep your back row as long as you can

Your back row is where your opponent's pieces become kings. If you keep a couple of pieces there, especially on the squares that guard it best, it's very hard for them to crown. Many strong players leave two back-row pieces in place until late in the game.

3. Control the centre

Pieces in the middle of the board can go both ways and support each other. Pieces on the sides can only move one way and are easier to trap. Aim to get your pieces into the centre early.

4. Move in groups

A lonely piece far ahead of the others is easy to capture. Move pieces so they protect each other diagonally. A piece with a friend right behind it can't be jumped from that side.

5. Trade when you're ahead

If you have more pieces, swapping one for one is good for you: 8 against 7 becomes 3 against 2, and then 2 against 1, where your extra piece decides the game. If you're behind, avoid trades and look for a trap instead.

6. Get a king, then use it

Kings can move backwards, which makes them much better at hunting single pieces. Once you have one, use it to attack from behind while your normal pieces push forward.

7. Look one jump ahead

Before every move, ask: "After this, can my opponent jump?" and "Can I force a double jump next turn?" Most games against the computer are lost to one jump that wasn't seen coming.

Practise

Checkers on the Hub has easy and hard computer opponents and a two-player mode. Against Hard, try to set up a forced jump that leads into your own multi-jump. It's the most satisfying move in the game.
