From Pong to phones: a short history of arcade games

By Noam · 15 November 2026 · 8 min read

A lot of the games on this site have a long family history. When you play Snake, Block Drop or Space Rocks, you're playing a modern version of an idea that's decades old. Here's a quick tour of where they came from.

Before arcades

Some of the first video games were made in science labs. In 1952, A. S. Douglas made a computer version of noughts and crosses called OXO at the University of Cambridge. In 1958, physicist William Higinbotham built Tennis for Two on an oscilloscope for visitors to his lab. These games weren't sold; they were experiments and demonstrations.

The 1970s: Pong starts an industry

In 1972, Atari released Pong, a simple tennis game with two paddles and a square ball. It was a huge hit in bars and arcades, and it showed that people would pay to play video games. Our Paddle Battle is a direct descendant.

Atari followed with Breakout in 1976, where you bounce a ball to break a wall of bricks, the idea behind Brick Smash. In the same year, an arcade game called Blockade had players leaving trails behind them, an early ancestor of Snake.

1978 to 1981: the golden age

Taito's Space Invaders arrived in 1978 and was a sensation. Rows of aliens marched down the screen, and players defended the Earth from below. Space Shooter keeps that idea and adds aliens that dive at you, like later games such as Galaga.

In 1979, Atari's Asteroids gave players a spaceship that drifted realistically through a field of rocks, the inspiration for Space Rocks. Then, in 1980, Namco's Pac-Man introduced a hungry yellow hero and four ghosts that each chase you in their own way. Its designer, Toru Iwatani, wanted a game that everyone would enjoy, not just fans of shooting games. You can try our take in Maze Muncher.

In 1981, Donkey Kong made jumping the main action of a game. Its hero later became Mario, and platform games like Pixel Jumper have been popular ever since.

The 1980s: puzzles and home consoles

Games moved from arcades into homes. In 1984, Alexey Pajitnov created Tetris in Moscow, and in 1989 it came bundled with Nintendo's Game Boy, which made it one of the best-known games ever. Block Drop follows the same rules. Bubble Bobble in 1986 was loved for letting two players work together, the inspiration for Bubble Blaster.

Computers at work: Solitaire and Minesweeper

In the early 1990s, Windows came with Solitaire and Minesweeper. They helped people get comfortable with a mouse, and they became some of the most-played games in the world. Try them here as Solitaire and Mine Finder.

Phones and browsers

In 1997, Nokia put Snake on its phones, and for many people it was the first game they played on a mobile. When smartphones arrived, simple touch games exploded: Doodle Jump (2009), Fruit Ninja (2010) and Flappy Bird (2013) were played by millions. You'll find their spirit in Sky Climber, Fruit Slash and Flappy Chicken.

Meanwhile, web browsers became powerful enough to run games without plug-ins. In 2014, Gabriele Cirulli released 2048 as a free web page and it went around the world in weeks. The same year, Google Chrome hid a dinosaur game on its offline page, which inspired Dino Run.

Why the old ideas still work

The classics share something: you understand them in seconds, but mastering them takes much longer. There's no long tutorial, just a simple rule and a score to beat. That's why these ideas keep coming back, and why I keep making new versions of them. If you want to see how a game like this is built, read How a browser game works.
