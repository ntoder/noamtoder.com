Learn to touch type: a simple guide for kids and beginners

By Noam · 15 November 2026 · 6 min read

Touch typing means typing with all ten fingers without looking at the keyboard. It feels slow and awkward for the first week, and then something clicks. People who touch type often type two or three times faster than people who hunt for keys with two fingers. When you're learning to code, being able to type well means you spend your time thinking about the code instead of searching for the semicolon.

Start on the home row

Every finger has a home. Put your left fingers on A S D F and your right fingers on J K L ;. Your thumbs rest on the space bar. Feel the small bumps on the F and J keys: they're there so you can find home without looking.

From home, each finger reaches up and down to nearby keys. Your left index finger, for example, handles F and also R, T, G, V and B. After every key, your fingers return to the home row.

Don't look down

This is the hardest rule and the most important one. Every time you look at the keyboard, you teach your brain to rely on your eyes instead of your fingers. If you have to, put a piece of paper over your hands. You'll make more mistakes at first, and that's fine. Mistakes are how your fingers learn.

Accuracy first, speed later

It's tempting to go fast, but speed built on mistakes doesn't last. Type slowly enough that you hit the right key nearly every time. Speed comes by itself when your fingers know where to go. A good beginner target is 15 to 20 words per minute with very few errors. Many adults type around 40.

Practise little and often

Ten minutes a day is much better than an hour once a week. Your fingers build memory through repetition, and short sessions keep you from getting tired and sloppy. Two weeks of daily practice makes a big difference.

Make it a game

That's where games help. In Typing Rain, words fall toward a little city and you type them to blast them before they land. It's built to train the right habits:

  • You have to type accurately, because wrong letters waste time.
  • You have to keep your eyes on the screen to see which word is lowest.
  • Every ten words, the level goes up with longer, faster words, so the challenge grows with you.

Try to keep your fingers on the home row the whole game, even when it gets busy.

Sit comfortably

Good posture makes typing easier and stops your hands getting tired. Sit up straight with your feet flat, keep your wrists level rather than bent, and have the screen at about eye level. Take a short break every now and then to stretch your fingers.

A bit of history

The QWERTY layout we use today was designed for typewriters in the 1870s, named after the first six letters on the top row. Many other layouts have been invented since, but QWERTY stuck, so it's the one worth learning.
