Five in a Row (Gomoku) strategy: open threes, fours and forks

By Noam · 26 November 2026 · 6 min read

Five in a Row, also called Gomoku, is played on a 15 by 15 grid. Players take turns placing stones, and the first to get five in a straight line (across, down or diagonal) wins. It's like Tic-Tac-Toe on a huge board, but much deeper. The game is centuries old in Japan, and it's closely related to Renju, a version with extra rules to make it fairer.

The shapes to know

  • Open two: two stones in a line with space on both sides. A starting point.
  • Open three: three in a line with both ends open. Very dangerous: if it isn't blocked, it becomes an open four.
  • Four: four in a line with one end open. Must be blocked immediately.
  • Open four: four in a line with both ends open. It can't be stopped, because blocking one end leaves the other.

Rule 1: block open threes

An open three is the most important thing to notice on the board. If your opponent makes one and you don't block an end, they'll turn it into an open four and win. Unless you can make a four yourself (which forces them to answer you first), block it.

Rule 2: attack with forcing moves

A move that makes a four or an open three forces your opponent to respond. Strong players chain forcing moves together, so the opponent spends every turn blocking and never gets to attack. If you can keep forcing, you control the game.

Rule 3: build a fork

The winning idea in Gomoku is the double threat: one stone that creates two threats at once, for example a four and an open three, or two open threes. Your opponent can only block one. Look for a spot where two of your lines cross; that's where forks come from.

Rule 4: stay connected

Stones placed close together support each other and can join several lines at once. Stones spread out across the board rarely help each other. Play near your existing stones, usually within one or two squares.

Rule 5: defend by attacking

When you have to block, choose the blocking square that also builds one of your own lines. The best defensive moves are also attacking moves.

Is it fair?

Going first is a big advantage. Computer research in the 1990s showed that on a standard board the first player can force a win with perfect play. That's why the professional version, Renju, gives the first player some restrictions. For a friendly game it doesn't matter much, but it's a good reason to take turns going first.

Practise

Five in a Row on the Hub has a computer opponent and a two-player mode. Play a game where you say "open three!" every time either player makes one. You'll quickly start seeing the board the way strong players do.
