The real Moon landing behind Moon Lander

By Noam · 26 November 2026 · 7 min read

In Moon Lander you fire your engine carefully to touch down gently before your fuel runs out. It's a game, but it's based on something real. On 20 July 1969, the Apollo 11 lunar module, called Eagle, landed on the Moon with astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin on board, while Michael Collins orbited above in the command module. The landing was much closer to a game of Moon Lander than most people realise.

Why you can't just fall

The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's, so things fall more slowly there. But it has almost no air, which means there's nothing for a parachute to grab. The only way to slow down is to fire a rocket engine against your fall. Burn too little and you crash; burn too much and you waste fuel and hover. That's exactly the balance you have to find in the game.

Alarms on the way down

During the descent, Eagle's guidance computer showed alarms with the codes 1202 and 1201. The computer was being asked to do more work than it had time for. Engineers in Mission Control quickly decided the computer was still doing its most important jobs, and told the crew to carry on. The computer had been designed to drop less important tasks when it was overloaded, which is exactly what it did.

Flying by hand

As they got close to the surface, Armstrong saw that the computer was guiding them towards a crater surrounded by large boulders. He took manual control and flew the lander forward, looking for a smoother spot. All the while, the fuel was running low. By the time Eagle touched down, it had less than a minute of landing fuel to spare.

"The Eagle has landed"

After touching down in an area called the Sea of Tranquillity, Armstrong radioed: "Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed." A few hours later he became the first person to step onto the Moon.

The early Lunar Lander games

The Moon landings inspired one of the earliest computer games. In 1969 a high school student named Jim Storer wrote a text-only version, where players typed in how much fuel to burn each second. Arcade and home computer versions with graphics followed in the late 1970s, and the idea has been remade countless times since.

Tips for the game

  • Short bursts: tap the engine instead of holding it, like Armstrong flying carefully.
  • Slow down early: it's easier to slow a lander from high up than to stop a fast fall at the last moment.
  • Watch your sideways speed: landing gently but sliding sideways still breaks the legs.
  • Pick your spot: just like Apollo 11, look for flat ground before you're too low to move.

Play

Try to land with fuel to spare in Moon Lander, and remember that real astronauts had to do it for real, with no restart button.
