Healthy gaming: a simple guide for kids and parents

By Noam · 26 November 2026 · 6 min read

Games can be a great part of growing up. They can teach problem-solving, maths, typing, teamwork and patience, and they're fun. Like most good things, they work best in balance with everything else. This guide has simple, practical tips for kids and parents.

For kids

Take breaks

Every half hour or so, stand up, stretch, look at something far away and get a drink of water. Your eyes and your body will thank you. If a game makes you feel frustrated or angry, that's a great moment for a break too. Games are more fun when you come back fresh.

Games after homework, not before bed

Screens and exciting games right before sleep can make it harder to fall asleep. Try to finish games a while before bedtime, and keep phones and tablets out of the bedroom at night if you can.

Stay safe online

  • Never share your full name, address, school or phone number in a game or chat.
  • Use a nickname, not your real name.
  • If someone online is mean, asks for personal information or makes you uncomfortable, stop, don't reply, and tell a parent or teacher.
  • Never share passwords, even with friends.

Be a good player

Win nicely, lose nicely, and be kind in comments. Everyone was a beginner once.

For parents

Make a plan together

Health organisations such as the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend that families agree on screen-time rules together, rather than just setting limits. A family media plan can cover when and where screens are used, and what comes first (homework, chores, outside time). Children are more likely to follow rules they helped make.

Very young children

The World Health Organization recommends no sedentary screen time for children under two, and no more than one hour a day for children aged two to four, with less being better. For older children, the focus is less on exact minutes and more on making sure screens don't push out sleep, physical activity, schoolwork and time with family.

Play together

Playing a game with your child is one of the best ways to know what they're playing and to talk about it. Two-player games on the same screen are good for this: many games on this site have a "2 Players" mode.

Choose games carefully

Look for games without violence, without pressure to buy things, and without open chat with strangers. On this site, every game can be played free in the browser, ads are set to child-safe mode, and accounts are optional.

Signs it might be too much

It might be time to rebalance if gaming regularly gets in the way of sleep, homework, meals or friendships, or if stopping always ends in big arguments. If you're worried, a family doctor or school counsellor is a good person to talk to.

The good side

Games don't have to be the bad guy. Puzzle games build patience and logic, word games build vocabulary, and making games, like in our guide to making your first game, can be the start of a real interest in coding. Balance is the key.
