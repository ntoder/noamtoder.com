Rhythm, beats and BPM: the music behind rhythm games

By Noam · 26 November 2026 · 6 min read

Rhythm games ask you to tap in time with music, and beat makers let you build your own patterns. Both are great ways to learn how rhythm works in real music. Here are the basics.

The beat

The beat is the steady pulse you tap your foot to. It doesn't stop when the drums do; it's like an invisible clock underneath the music. Rhythm games are built around this clock: every note you hit lines up with a beat or a fraction of one.

BPM: how fast is the beat?

BPM means beats per minute. A slow song might be around 70 BPM, a pop song often between 100 and 130, and fast dance music can be 170 or more. A resting human heart beats roughly 60 to 100 times a minute, which might be one reason songs in that range feel natural.

Counting in fours

Most songs group beats into bars (also called measures) of four beats: 1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 2, 3, 4. This is called 4/4 time. Waltzes count in threes (3/4 time), which is why they sound like they sway. Next time you hear a song, try counting along to find where the "1" is; it's usually where the music feels strongest.

Splitting the beat

Each beat can be split into halves (eighth notes) or quarters (sixteenth notes). Many beat makers, including Beat Maker on this site, use a grid of 16 steps: one bar of four beats, each split into four. Each column is one tiny slice of time, and each row is a sound.

Your first drum pattern

Here's a basic pattern that sits underneath thousands of songs:

  • Kick drum (the deep "boom") on steps 1 and 9: beats 1 and 3.
  • Snare or clap (the sharp "crack") on steps 5 and 13: beats 2 and 4.
  • Hi-hat (the "tss") on every other step: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15.

Play it back and you'll recognise it straight away. Now try moving one kick drum to step 11, or adding hi-hats on every step, and listen to how the feeling changes.

Syncopation

When a sound lands between the main beats instead of on them, it's called syncopation. It's what makes music feel bouncy or funky. In a beat maker, try putting a kick or snare on an even-numbered step and you'll hear it.

Getting better at rhythm games

  • Listen to the music, don't just watch the notes. Your ears are often more accurate than your eyes.
  • Tap your foot or nod along to keep the beat inside you.
  • Stay relaxed. Tense fingers are slower and less accurate.
  • Practise slower songs first; timing matters more than speed.
