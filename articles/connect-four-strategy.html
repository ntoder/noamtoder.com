How to win at Four in a Row: centre columns, traps and odd rows

By Noam · 25 November 2026 · 6 min read

Four in a Row (often sold as Connect Four) looks simple: drop discs into a 7-column board and be the first to line up four. But there is a lot of strategy hiding in it. In 1988 two researchers, James D. Allen and Victor Allis, separately proved that the first player can always win with perfect play, as long as they start in the middle column. You don't need perfect play to beat a friend or the computer, though. These ideas will take you a long way.

1. Own the middle

The centre column is part of more possible lines of four than any other column. A disc on the edge can only be part of a few lines, but a disc in the middle can join horizontal, vertical and both diagonal lines in many ways. That's why strong players open in the centre and keep coming back to it. If you're second, putting your first disc on top of your opponent's middle disc is a good, solid reply.

2. Count lines, not discs

Before each move, look at where you could still make four. A disc that is blocked in every direction is almost useless. A disc that sits in several open lines is strong. A quick way to compare two moves is to ask: "How many different fours could this disc still be part of?"

3. Always check for the opponent's threat first

Most games between beginners are lost the same way: someone is busy with their own plan and misses that the other player has three in a row with an open end. Make it a habit: before you choose your move, scan the board for any place your opponent could win next turn. Blocking comes first, every time.

4. Don't play under a winning square

Because discs fall to the bottom, a square can only be filled when the one below it is full. If your opponent needs a certain square to win, don't put a disc right underneath it, because you'd be handing it to them. Good players use this the other way round too: they create a threat high up and wait until the opponent is forced to fill the square below.

5. Make two threats at once

A single threat is easy to block. Two threats are not, because your opponent only gets one move. The classic shape is a "7": three discs in a row plus a diagonal that shares a disc, so one move creates two ways to win. Another strong pattern is two threats stacked on top of each other in the same column. When your opponent blocks the lower one, it lets you play the upper one.

6. The odd and even row rule

Number the rows from the bottom, 1 to 6. Over a whole game the first player tends to get the odd rows and the second player the even rows, because the board fills up in pairs. So if you went first, threats on rows 3 and 5 are especially valuable. If you went second, threats on rows 2, 4 and 6 are the ones that usually pay off at the end. This is the idea Victor Allis used to solve the game, and it's surprisingly useful in normal play once the board is half full.

7. Slow down at the end

When only a few columns are still open, the game becomes a counting puzzle. Count how many empty squares are left in each column and work out who will be forced to play where. Often the winner isn't the player with more threats, but the one who makes the other run out of safe moves.

Practise against the computer
