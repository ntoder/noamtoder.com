Chess for beginners: 10 rules that win your first games

By Noam · 15 November 2026 · 7 min read

When beginners play chess, the games are almost never decided by clever plans. They're decided by a piece left where it can be taken for free. The good news is that you don't need to study openings for years to start winning. A handful of rules fixes most beginner mistakes. Here are the ten I teach first.

1. Control the centre

The four squares in the middle of the board are the most important. A knight in the centre can reach eight squares; a knight in the corner can reach two. Start the game by moving the pawn in front of your king or queen two squares forward. It claims the centre and opens lines for your bishops and queen.

2. Develop your pieces before attacking

"Developing" means moving your knights and bishops off the back row to useful squares. Try to bring out all four before you go hunting. A beginner's attack with just the queen usually fails, because the other pieces aren't there to help.

3. Don't move the same piece twice in the opening

Every move spent moving a piece again is a move not spent bringing out a new one. There are exceptions, such as when a piece is attacked, but as a habit, use each opening move to develop something new.

4. Castle early

Castling moves your king to the side, behind a wall of pawns, and brings a rook toward the middle in one move. A king left in the centre is a target once the board opens up. Most strong players castle within the first ten moves.

5. Don't bring your queen out too early

The queen is your most powerful piece, which also makes her the easiest to chase. If she comes out early, your opponent can develop their pieces while attacking her, and you waste moves running away.

6. Before every move, ask: what is my opponent threatening?

This single question would save most lost games. Look at the move your opponent just made. Which of your pieces does it attack? Is there a check, a capture or a threat coming? Only then think about your own plan.

7. Check that your move doesn't leave something hanging

A "hanging" piece is one that can be captured for free. Before you let go of a piece, look at the square it's going to and the square it's leaving. Can it be taken? Did moving it leave another piece unprotected?

8. Count before you trade

When pieces are attacking the same square, count how many of yours attack it and how many of theirs defend it. If you have more attackers, the trade usually goes your way. Remember the rough values: pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9. Trading a knight for a rook is a good deal; trading a rook for a knight is not.

9. Look for forks

A fork is one move that attacks two things at once. Knights are brilliant at forks because they jump. If you can attack the king and the queen at the same time, your opponent can only save one. Look for squares where a knight would attack two valuable pieces.

10. In the endgame, use your king

When most of the pieces are gone, the king stops being something to hide and becomes a fighter. Walk it toward the centre and use it to support your pawns. A pawn that reaches the far side becomes a queen, so in the endgame, every pawn matters.

How to practise

Our Chess game has three levels. Start on Easy and try to use all ten rules in every game. Easy makes mistakes you can punish, which teaches you to spot hanging pieces. Use Undo when you blunder and ask yourself which rule you broke. When you win comfortably, move up. Hard looks several moves ahead, and beating it means your habits are solid.
