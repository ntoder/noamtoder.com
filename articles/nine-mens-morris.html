Nine Men's Morris: an ancient game and how to win it

By Noam · 25 November 2026 · 6 min read

Nine Men's Morris is one of the oldest board games still played. Boards for it have been found scratched into stone from Roman times, and it was so common in medieval England that Shakespeare mentions it in A Midsummer Night's Dream, where a "nine men's morris" board cut into a village green has filled up with mud. The board is three squares, one inside the other, joined by lines, with 24 points where pieces can stand.

The rules

Each player has nine pieces. Getting three of your pieces in a straight line along a line of the board is called a mill, and every time you make one you remove one of your opponent's pieces. The game has three phases:

  1. Placing: players take turns putting their nine pieces on empty points.
  2. Moving: once all pieces are on the board, you move one piece along a line to a neighbouring empty point.
  3. Flying: when a player is down to three pieces, they can move a piece to any empty point.

You lose if you have only two pieces left, or if you can't make a move.

Placing phase: think about moving

Beginners rush to make mills while placing. Good players think ahead to the moving phase. Pieces on the corners of the squares have only two neighbours, so they're easy to block. The points in the middle of each side, especially on the middle square, have three or four neighbours and give you much more freedom later.

Block early, block often

If your opponent has two in a line with the third point empty, block it unless you can make a mill yourself first. Losing a piece early is a big deal, because fewer pieces means fewer ways to move.

The "running mill"

The best shape in the moving phase is a mill where one piece can step out and back in again. Each time it steps back in, the mill is made again and you remove another piece. Even better is a "double mill", where one piece moving back and forth closes a mill on both sides, removing a piece every single turn. If you can build one, the game is usually over.

Keep your opponent stuck

Since a player who can't move loses, crowding your opponent's pieces into corners is a real winning plan, even if you never make a mill.

Flying changes everything

When someone has three pieces, they can jump anywhere, which makes them very dangerous: they can block almost any mill and threaten new ones. If you're the one with more pieces, close mills quickly and avoid leaving two-in-a-line shapes with an empty third point.
