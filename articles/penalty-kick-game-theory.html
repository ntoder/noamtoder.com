Penalty kicks and game theory: why being unpredictable wins

By Noam · 26 November 2026 · 6 min read

A penalty kick looks like a test of skill, and it is. But it's also a guessing game. The ball travels so fast that a goalkeeper usually has to choose which way to dive before seeing where the ball goes. That makes a penalty a perfect example of something mathematicians call game theory: the study of decisions where your best choice depends on what the other person chooses.

Why you can't have a favourite side

Imagine a striker who always shoots to the left because it's their strongest side. Soon every keeper knows, dives left, and saves it. Now imagine a keeper who always dives right. Strikers would just shoot left every time. Any fixed habit can be exploited. The only way to avoid being predictable is to mix: sometimes left, sometimes right, sometimes the middle, in a way the other player can't guess.

The best mix isn't 50/50

Most players have a stronger side. If you're more accurate shooting to your natural side, you should shoot there more often, but not always. The best mix is the one where the keeper gains nothing by guessing one way. Game theory calls this a mixed strategy, and the balanced point is called an equilibrium, named after the mathematician John Nash.

Economists have studied thousands of professional penalties, and found that top players' choices come surprisingly close to the balance game theory predicts. Without doing any maths, the best players have learned to be just unpredictable enough.

What about the middle?

Goalkeepers dive to one side most of the time, because standing still and being beaten looks bad. That means shots straight down the middle are saved surprisingly rarely. But if everyone started shooting down the middle, keepers would stay put. The middle works best as an occasional surprise, which is exactly what game theory says.

Using this in the game

  • When you shoot: don't fall into a pattern. If you've gone left twice, the computer may expect left again, or may expect you to switch. Keep it truly mixed.
  • When you're in goal: watch for patterns in the computer's shots, but don't overreact to one or two kicks. Random sequences often have runs that look like patterns.
  • Aim for the corners. Shots near the corners are hardest to save, but miss the goal more often. Balance accuracy against risk.

Game theory is everywhere

The same idea of mixing your choices shows up in Rock, Paper, Scissors, in tennis serves, in card games like poker, and in many computer games where the enemy tries to predict you. Whenever an opponent is trying to guess your next move, the safest plan is to be hard to guess.
